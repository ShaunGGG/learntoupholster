What's the best upholstery fabric if you have a dog?

Last updated: 8 August 2026

Blog · Fabrics

What upholstery fabric is best for dogs?

A tight, flat weave with no loops, in a fibre that tolerates cleaning. In practice that means a densely woven polyester or performance fabric, a solution-dyed acrylic, a microfibre, or a good pigmented leather if you would rather deal with scratches than hair. Ask for a Martindale rating of at least 40,000 rubs. The thing to avoid above all else is loop pile — bouclé especially — because any loop a claw fits into eventually gets pulled into a run. Fibre matters, but weave structure matters more, and no fabric on earth is dog-proof.

People usually ask this question the wrong way round. They ask which fabric is best, when the thing that actually determines whether a sofa survives a dog is how the cloth is constructed. A magnificent hard-wearing yarn woven into loops will be destroyed faster than a modest yarn woven flat.

So start there, and worry about fibre second.

Rule one: no loops. None.

If a claw can get into a loop, sooner or later it will, and when the dog pulls its foot back the loop comes with it. One pulled loop becomes a run, and a run cannot be repaired invisibly.

This rules out the fabric everybody currently wants. Bouclé is the single worst cloth you can put on a sofa in a house with a dog or a cat — it is nothing but loops, that is the entire aesthetic. It looks wonderful in a photograph of a room nobody lives in.

Treat the same way anything described as looped, textured with raised loops, or a heavy nubby weave you can catch a fingernail in. That is the test, incidentally: run a fingernail across the sample with some pressure. If it catches or lifts anything, walk away.

Chenille is a gentler case — the pile is cut rather than looped, so it does not run in the same way, but it is soft and it holds hair enthusiastically. Tolerable, not ideal.

Rule two: tight and flat wins

A dense, flat weave gives a claw nothing to work with and gives hair nothing to bury into. It is also easier to vacuum, which matters more day to day than any of this.

Martindale rub rating is a reasonable proxy for density, though not a complete one. Using the same bands as the rest of the site: 25,000 is general domestic, 40,000 is heavy domestic or light contract, 100,000 is severe contract. With a dog, treat 40,000 as your floor, and do not be shy about asking for contract-grade cloth in a domestic house. Pubs specify it because it survives.

Fibre by fibre, honestly

  • Polyester and "performance" fabrics. The sensible default. Doesn't absorb liquid readily, tolerates cleaning, resists abrasion, holds colour. Most of the fabrics marketed for family homes are polyester or a polyester blend, and for good reason.
  • Solution-dyed acrylic. Made for outdoor furniture, increasingly specified indoors. The colour is locked into the fibre rather than applied to it, so many of them can be cleaned with dilute bleach without fading. Extraordinarily tough. If you have a dog that swims and then sits down, this is your fabric.
  • Microfibre. Very tightly woven fine polyester. Claws find nothing to grip, liquid sits on the surface long enough to blot, and hair brushes off. Unglamorous and genuinely excellent.
  • Polypropylene. Highly stain resistant and cheap. Feels like what it is, but it works.
  • Wool. Better than people expect — naturally soil-shedding, springy, recovers from crushing, and it does not hold smells the way cotton does. It can felt where a dog lies repeatedly, and it costs. A good option if the budget allows and the dog is civilised.
  • Cotton and linen. Both absorb everything: water, mud, drool, smell. Linen also snags and creases. Fine as washable loose covers, poor as fixed upholstery in a dog house.
  • Viscose velvet. No. It marks permanently when damp, and a wet dog is damp. See why velvet goes patchy.
  • Polyester velvet. Genuinely decent, which surprises people. Short dense pile, no loops, hair brushes off in the pile direction, and it tolerates cleaning. It will show pressure marks where the dog lies, and you have to decide whether that bothers you.

What about leather?

Leather is a completely different trade-off rather than a better one.

What you gain: hair does not embed itself, it sits on the surface and wipes off. Spills wipe off. It does not hold smell. For a shedding breed this is a substantial daily advantage.

What you give up: claws leave scratches, and on some finishes they are permanent. There is no weave to protect the surface.

If you go leather, the grade decides everything. Pigmented or corrected-grain leather has a protective surface finish, hides light scratching best, and can be recoloured by a specialist when it does mark. Aniline leather is dyed through with no protective coat — beautiful, and it will record every claw. Bonded leather and faux leather are the worst possible choice, because a claw punctures the coating and the coating then peels, which is a thing no repair fixes. That is covered properly in why leather peels.

The colour trick nobody mentions

Match the fabric to the dog.

It sounds flippant and it is the most practically useful thing in this article. A black sofa with a golden retriever is a permanent hair exhibition. A mid-tone flecked cloth in roughly the dog's colour hides an astonishing amount.

Beyond that: mid-tones beat both extremes, because very dark shows hair and dust and very light shows everything else. Patterns with tonal variation, tweeds, flecks and heathered weaves disguise both hair and small marks far better than a flat solid colour. Solid pale grey is the least forgiving cloth commonly sold.

Construction choices that matter as much as the cloth

  • Removable cushion covers with zips. The single best decision you can make. Covers come off, get cleaned, go back. Check they are properly zipped rather than sewn closed before you commit.
  • Loose covers over the whole piece. Washable, replaceable, and the frame underneath stays pristine. Less tailored looking, and some people cannot live with that. See loose covers.
  • A throw where the dog actually lies. Dogs are creatures of habit and will use the same spot. A washable sacrificial layer on that spot does more than any fabric choice.
  • Arm caps. Old-fashioned, and they exist because arms wear out first.

Test the sample before you commit

Get a decent-sized cutting, not a postage stamp, and do three things to it. Drag a key or a fingernail across it firmly and see if anything lifts. Put a few drops of water on it and see whether they sit or soak. Rub it hard with a cloth twenty times and see whether the surface changes.

Two minutes of that tells you more than any label. If you want to see how a cloth will actually look on your piece first, the fabric visualiser will show you before you buy, and our own fabric shop at Bodella will send samples.

The honest summary

There is no dog-proof fabric, and anyone selling you one is selling you something. What there is, is a range that runs from "this will be ruined within a year" to "this will look fine for a decade with sensible care", and the distance between those two is mostly decided by whether the cloth has loops in it.

If somebody asked me for one answer with no further conversation: a mid-tone flecked polyester or performance weave, at least 40,000 Martindale, with zipped removable cushion covers and a throw on the dog's favourite end.

And if the dog is going to be on the sofa regardless — and it is — then choosing a cloth that survives it is a great deal more sensible than fighting about it for the next ten years.

Common questions

What is the most hard-wearing upholstery fabric for dogs?

A tightly woven polyester, performance weave, microfibre or solution-dyed acrylic with a Martindale rating of at least 40,000 rubs. Solution-dyed acrylic is the toughest of the group and many types can be cleaned with dilute bleach without losing colour. Whatever the fibre, the weave must be flat with no loops, because loops are what claws destroy.

Is bouclé a bad choice with pets?

Yes, it is close to the worst choice available. Bouclé is constructed entirely from loops, and a claw that catches a loop pulls it into a run that cannot be repaired invisibly. The same applies to any heavily looped or nubby weave. Run a fingernail firmly across a sample: if anything lifts or catches, it will not survive a dog or a cat.

Is leather good for dog owners?

It is a trade-off rather than an upgrade. Hair does not embed in leather and wipes off, spills wipe off and it holds no smell, which is a real daily advantage with a shedding breed. Against that, claws scratch it and there is no weave to protect the surface. Pigmented or corrected-grain leather hides scratching best and can be recoloured; aniline records everything; bonded and faux leather should be avoided entirely, because a punctured coating goes on to peel.

What colour sofa hides dog hair best?

A mid-tone in roughly the colour of your dog, with some tonal variation in the weave. Flecks, tweeds and heathered cloths disguise hair and small marks far better than flat solid colours. Very dark fabrics show light hair and dust, very pale fabrics show everything else, and plain pale grey is the least forgiving cloth in common use.
