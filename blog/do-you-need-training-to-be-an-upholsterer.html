Do you need formal training to become an upholsterer?

Last updated: 12 August 2026

Blog · Learning the Trade

Do you need a qualification to work as an upholsterer?

No. Upholstery is an unregulated trade in the UK — there is no licence, no mandatory qualification and no register. You could take work on tomorrow morning. But being allowed to and being ready to are different things, and there are legal obligations, fire safety in particular, that apply to you from the first job whether anyone taught you about them or not. What training really buys is time: it compresses years of expensive mistakes into months, and it gives you the things you would never think to look up because you did not know they existed.

I get asked this by career changers more than anything else, usually phrased slightly apologetically, as though there were an obvious right answer they have missed.

There isn't one. But there is a lot more to say than yes or no.

The legal position, plainly

Upholstery is unregulated. No licence, no registration, no mandatory qualification, no protected title. Anyone in the UK can describe themselves as an upholsterer and start charging for it this week, and a good number of people do.

What is not optional is the law that applies to the work itself, and this is where self-taught upholsterers most often come unstuck — not through carelessness, but because nobody told them the obligation existed.

The Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations apply to reupholstery work. They govern what fillings and coverings you may use, when an interliner is required, and what happens when a customer supplies their own fabric. Breaching them is a criminal matter, not a paperwork slip. Our fire safety checker will tell you what applies to a given job, and if any of that is news to you, that is precisely the argument for structured training.

The single most common gap in a self-taught upholsterer is not skill. It is not knowing that fire regulations apply to them at all.

What training actually gives you

The order of operations. A surprising amount of this craft is sequence. Do things in the wrong order and you will produce something that looks acceptable and is impossible to finish properly, and you will not understand why. Sequence is very hard to reverse-engineer from a finished piece.

The traditional skills you would never work out alone. Stitched edges, spring tying, the correct tension on webbing, how a scroll arm is actually built. You can watch a stitched edge being made and still not be able to make one. These are hand skills that need somebody standing next to you saying "no, like this".

A feedback loop. This is the real value and it is the thing videos cannot replicate. Working alone, you find out a technique was wrong roughly three years later when a chair fails in a customer's house. In a workshop with a tutor, you find out in four minutes, before you have built anything on top of the error.

Estimating. How much cloth a piece takes, how long a job takes, what to charge. Get this wrong self-employed and no amount of craft skill saves you.

Speed. The difference between an enthusiastic amateur and a professional is very often not quality — it is hours. A hobbyist can produce beautiful work in three weeks. Doing it in two days at the same standard is the trade.

What training does not give you

Worth being even-handed, because courses are sold hard and they are not magic.

  • It does not make you fast. Speed comes from repetition, and repetition happens after the course, not during it.
  • It rarely teaches you the business. Quoting, chasing money, dealing with a customer who has changed their mind, deciding what work to turn down. Our Business Hub exists because that gap is real.
  • It does not replace hours. Nobody leaves a diploma course as a finished upholsterer. You leave knowing how to practise correctly, which is a different and more useful thing.
  • It does not guarantee customers. That is a separate discipline entirely.

The routes, roughly

  • AMUSF diplomas. The Association of Master Upholsterers and Soft Furnishers runs staged diploma qualifications delivered through approved training centres. Stage 1 covers foundations, with later stages moving into traditional and more advanced work. This is the recognised route in the trade.
  • College courses. Part-time and evening provision, varying a great deal by area and by tutor. Ask who is teaching it and what they did before teaching.
  • Private short courses. A weekend on a drop-in seat, a week on a chair. Excellent for finding out whether you like it. Not a route to a trade on their own.
  • Apprenticeships. Genuinely valuable and genuinely scarce. Worth pursuing if you can find one, and worth approaching workshops directly rather than waiting for one to be advertised.
  • Self-taught. Books, video, and taking old chairs apart. Entirely viable — with a caveat below.

Does the qualification matter commercially?

Honest answer: it depends entirely who you want to work for.

Domestic customers mostly do not know what the qualifications are. They judge you on photographs of your work, on how you speak to them and on whether somebody recommended you. A diploma will not win you a dining chair job.

Commercial and contract clients are a different world. Care homes, facilities management firms, hotel groups and insurers frequently ask for evidence of competence and compliance before they will put you on a list. Trade accreditation is a door-opener there, and that work is generally better paid and more regular than domestic.

So the qualification is worth least where beginners start and worth most where the sustainable money is. Which is worth knowing before you decide it does not matter.

The real risk of teaching yourself

It is not that self-taught work is bad. Plenty of self-taught upholsterers are excellent, and some conventions in this trade deserve to be questioned by people who did not absorb them uncritically.

The risk is narrower and sharper: you do not know what you do not know.

You can build something that looks entirely correct, photographs beautifully, and is wrong underneath. Wrong tension, wrong filling, wrong sequence, no interliner where one was needed. Nothing tells you. The customer is delighted. And then it fails in eighteen months, or worse, it fails a fire safety question you did not know you were required to answer.

Reading closes some of that gap — that is largely why I wrote the book, and why the whole of it is free to read on this site. But reading cannot tell you that the thing you are doing right now, with your hands, is wrong.

What I would actually advise

  • Curious, might be a hobby: do a short course on a small piece. Two days will tell you whether you love it. Do not spend thousands before you know.
  • Serious about trading: do a structured, staged qualification. Not because the certificate is magic, but because the sequence is taught properly and somebody corrects you. It will save you more than it costs.
  • Already trading, self-taught: consider a later-stage course rather than starting at the beginning. You will fill gaps you did not know were there, and it opens contract work.
  • Any of the above: learn the fire regulations first, this week, before your next job. It is the cheapest risk you will ever eliminate.

How long does it take?

To make a drop-in dining seat properly: an afternoon with somebody showing you.

To be trusted with a customer's stuffover chair: months.

To be quick enough to earn a living at it: a few years, and that is with steady work coming through the door.

To be genuinely good at traditional work: longer than that. I have been at this thirty years and pieces still arrive that make me think for a while before I start.

None of which should put anybody off. It is one of the few trades left where a thing you made with your hands leaves the workshop and outlives you. But go in knowing it is a long apprenticeship whether or not anybody calls it one — and that the choice is not really between trained and untrained. It is between learning it deliberately and learning it by accident.

Common questions

Do you need a qualification to be an upholsterer in the UK?

No. Upholstery is an unregulated trade with no licence, no mandatory qualification and no protected title, so anyone can begin trading immediately. However, legal obligations still apply to the work itself — the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations govern reupholstery, including when an interliner is required and how customer-supplied fabric is handled, and breaching them is a criminal matter rather than an administrative one.

How long does it take to train as an upholsterer?

A drop-in dining seat can be learned properly in an afternoon with someone showing you. Being trusted with a customer's stuffover chair takes months. Being fast enough to earn a living from it takes a few years of steady work, because speed comes from repetition rather than tuition. Traditional work — stitched edges, spring tying — takes considerably longer than that.

Are upholstery courses worth the money?

For anyone intending to trade, generally yes, because the order of operations is difficult to reverse-engineer alone and a tutor corrects an error in minutes rather than years. For someone unsure whether they enjoy it, a short two-day course is a far better first purchase than a long qualification. What courses do not provide is speed, business skills or customers, and any course sold on those grounds is overselling.

Can you teach yourself upholstery?

Yes, and many capable upholsterers are self-taught. The specific risk is not poor workmanship but not knowing what you have not learned — it is entirely possible to build a piece that looks correct, photographs well and is wrong underneath in tension, filling, sequence or fire compliance, with nothing to tell you until it fails. Reading closes part of that gap; being corrected while your hands are on the work closes the rest.
