Why does leather peel?

Last updated: 4 August 2026

Blog · Leather & Vinyl

Why is my leather sofa peeling?

Because it almost certainly is not leather. Genuine leather does not peel — it fades, softens, scuffs and eventually cracks, but it does not shed flakes from its surface. Peeling in sheets or flakes is the signature failure of bonded leather and of polyurethane-coated faux leather, where a thin plastic skin is separating from the backing underneath. There is one exception: heavily pigmented corrected-grain leather can lose its surface finish at high-wear points, which looks like peeling but leaves sound leather beneath and can be recoloured. Bonded and PU cannot be repaired. They can only be re-covered.

This is the single most common question I get from the public, and almost every answer online gets it wrong. People ring up furious with leather as a material, convinced they have been sold a dud hide. They have not. In nine cases out of ten they were never sold leather at all.

So it is worth being precise about what is actually happening on that sofa arm.

Leather does not peel. Plastic does.

A hide is a solid material all the way through. Colour is either dyed into it or sits on it as a thin pigment layer, but the substance beneath is one continuous thing. There is no separate skin to come away, because there is no separate skin.

What fails on a hide is different, and it looks different. Real leather dries out, loses suppleness, goes shiny where bodies rub it, fades in sunlight, and if it is left dry for long enough it cracks — splits that open into the material rather than lifting off it. Wear on genuine leather goes inward. Peeling goes outward.

When you can pick at an edge with a fingernail and lift a flake away, revealing a different-coloured fabric or fibrous mat underneath, you are looking at a coating that has stopped sticking to its backing. That is a manufactured laminate failing, and it is a completely different problem with a completely different answer.

The six things sold as "leather"

Part of the confusion is deliberate. The retail trade uses a set of terms that sound reassuring and mean very little. Here is what is actually on the shop floor, roughly best to worst for longevity.

  • Full-grain. The complete outer surface of the hide, nothing sanded away. Toughest fibre structure there is. Marks and darkens with use into a patina. Will not peel. Will outlive you if it is fed occasionally.
  • Top-grain. The grain surface lightly sanded to even out blemishes, then finished. The great majority of good upholstery leather. Excellent life. Will not peel.
  • Corrected-grain or pigmented. Heavily sanded, an artificial grain embossed in, then a substantial pigment and topcoat sprayed over. Real leather underneath, but the finish is doing the visual work. This one can flake at high-wear points — and it is the exception that makes this whole question confusing. More on it below.
  • Split. The lower layer of a hide after the grain has been taken off. No grain structure, so it is finished, embossed or brushed into suede. Weaker, and any applied finish sits on a fibrous surface with less to grip.
  • Bonded leather. Leather offcuts shredded into fibre, mixed with adhesive, spread onto a fabric or paper backing, and topped with a polyurethane layer embossed to look like grain. Leather content is often somewhere around a tenth to a fifth of the material. It is chipboard, in leather terms. This is the classic peeler.
  • Faux leather. Polyurethane or PVC coating on a woven or knitted textile backing. Sold as leatherette, pleather, PU, or lately as vegan leather. No hide involved at any point. The other classic peeler.

Watch for the phrase genuine leather on a swing tag. It sounds like a guarantee and functions as the opposite — it is the lowest grade term in common use, and it very often means a finished split. It tells you an animal was involved. It tells you nothing about how long the thing will last.

How to tell what you have got

You can usually settle this in about a minute, without any specialist kit.

  • Look at a cut edge. Under a cushion, at a seam, on the underside of a skirt. Real leather is the same material through its thickness, with a slightly fibrous, suede-like reverse. Bonded and faux show a clear sandwich: a coloured skin sitting on an obviously different backing, often a woven or knitted cloth.
  • Look at where it is failing. Peeling that starts at the seat front, the arm tops and the head area — precisely where bodies press and rub — and lifts in flakes is coating failure. Wear on real leather in those same places goes shiny and soft first.
  • Press it. Real leather warms to the hand quickly and creases into fine irregular lines that stay. Coated materials feel cooler for longer and fold in more uniform, springier creases.
  • Check the grain pattern. Embossed grain repeats. If you can find the same distinctive mark twice on different panels, it came off a roller, not an animal.
  • Smell it. Crude, but leather smells like leather and PU smells faintly of plastic. Less reliable on old pieces that have absorbed a room's worth of other smells.

Why the coating lets go

Polyurethane fails by hydrolysis. Moisture in ordinary household air slowly breaks down the polymer's chemical bonds, and it does so on a timetable that has very little to do with how carefully you treat the sofa. Warm, humid rooms accelerate it. The material is degrading from the day it is made, and eventually the layer loses both its integrity and its grip on the backing.

On bonded leather there is a second weakness. The adhesive holding the fibre mat to its backing is doing structural work it is not really built for, and every time somebody sits down, that laminate flexes. Flex a glued sandwich a few thousand times and it delaminates.

Then the ordinary business of being furniture speeds it all up:

  • Body oils and sweat. The head rest and arm tops go first on almost every piece, and it is not a coincidence — those are the contact points.
  • Sunlight and heat. A sofa against a radiator or in a bay window ages measurably faster at that end. I have seen pieces where you can read the sun's path across the back cushions.
  • The wrong cleaners. Baby wipes are the great destroyer. The surfactants in them strip finish, and people use them precisely because they are gentle on skin. Solvent cleaners and silicone polishes do their own damage.

In the workshop, bonded and PU pieces commonly start shedding somewhere between their second and fifth year. That is trade observation rather than a published figure, but it is remarkably consistent — and it is a fraction of what people expect when they buy something described as leather.

The exception: pigmented leather losing its finish

Here is where honest confusion is possible, because this genuinely is leather and it genuinely does flake.

Corrected-grain pigmented leather carries a thick sprayed finish. At the points that take the most abrasion — seat front edge, arm tops, the outer third of a seat cushion — that finish can crack and lift, leaving pale patches with ragged edges. It looks like peeling. Customers describe it as peeling.

The difference is what is underneath. Lift a flake of bonded leather and you find fabric backing. Lift a flake of failed pigment and you find leather — sound, if a different colour to the surface.

That distinction decides everything, because one of these is repairable and the other is not.

What can be repaired, honestly

I would rather tell someone the truth on the phone than take a deposit for work that will not hold.

  • Pigmented leather with finish loss: repairable. A leather refinishing specialist can clean back, fill, recolour and seal, and a good job lasts years. Worth doing on a decent frame.
  • Real leather that is dry, faded or lightly cracked: often improves a great deal with proper feeding and conditioning. Cracks that have not split right through can be stabilised.
  • Real leather split through, or torn: panel replacement. An upholsterer can cut and fit a new panel, though matching an aged hide is genuinely difficult and it is usually better to replace a whole section than to patch.
  • Bonded leather, peeling: not repairable. Nothing will bond reliably to a surface that is actively delaminating. Vinyl paints and repair kits will look acceptable for a few months and then lift along with everything else. Anyone promising otherwise is selling you a kit.
  • Faux leather, peeling: the same answer. Re-cover or replace.

The good news is that re-covering is very often worth it. If the frame is solid hardwood and the suspension is sound — which on a decent piece it usually is, even when the covering has failed early — then the expensive, hard-to-replace part of that sofa is still perfectly good. You are replacing the skin, not the animal.

Making real leather last

If you do have genuine leather, it wants very little, but it does want that little consistently.

  • Keep it out of direct sun and away from radiators. Heat and UV do more damage than use does.
  • Dust it and wipe it with a barely damp cloth. Nothing else, most of the time.
  • Feed it once or twice a year with a proper leather conditioner suited to the finish. Aniline and pigmented leathers want different products — ask before you buy.
  • Never use baby wipes, household cleaning sprays, solvent, or anything containing silicone.
  • Deal with spills immediately by blotting, not rubbing.

Do that and a good hide will look better at twenty years than it did at one.

What I tell people on the phone

When somebody rings about a peeling sofa, the first question is not what it costs. It is: can you go and pick at a flake and tell me what is underneath?

If it is fabric backing, I explain that they have bonded or faux leather, that nothing can be done to repair it, and that their choice is re-cover or replace. Most people are relieved to be given a straight answer, even a disappointing one — they have usually been told by three other people that a repair kit will sort it.

If it is leather underneath, I send them to a refinishing specialist, because that is a proper trade in its own right and they will do a better job of it than I will.

And if the frame is good and they want it re-covered, then we are having a much more enjoyable conversation about hides.

Common questions

Can peeling leather be repaired?

Only if it is real leather losing its surface finish, in which case a leather refinishing specialist can recolour and reseal it. If the flakes lift to reveal a fabric backing, it is bonded or faux leather and no repair will hold, because nothing bonds reliably to a delaminating surface. Repair kits on bonded leather look acceptable for a few months and then lift with everything else.

Is bonded leather real leather?

Only partly. It is made from shredded leather offcuts mixed with adhesive, spread on a fabric or paper backing and topped with an embossed polyurethane layer. Actual leather content is often around a tenth to a fifth of the material. It is legally describable as containing leather, but it behaves like a plastic laminate, not like a hide.

How long should a leather sofa last?

Good top-grain or full-grain leather on a sound frame should last decades and improve in appearance as it goes. Bonded and polyurethane faux leather commonly begin shedding within two to five years. If a sofa described as leather starts peeling early, the material is the reason, not the way it was treated.

Why is my sofa peeling only on the arms and headrest?

Because those are the contact points. Body oils, sweat and constant abrasion break down a surface coating fastest exactly where people touch it. On coated materials this shows as flaking; on genuine leather the same areas simply go shiny and soft first. The pattern of failure is itself a clue to which material you have.
