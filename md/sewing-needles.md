# Sewing machine needles for upholstery

*Sewing · Needles*

> Upholstery needle sizes by thread size, needle systems, round and leather points, and why a blunt needle causes skipped stitches.

Canonical: https://www.learntoupholster.com/sewing-needles

Needle size follows thread size. Get the pairing wrong and no amount of tension adjustment will save the seam.

**The principle.** The needle has to make a hole big enough for the thread to pass through twice — down and back — without chafing, but no bigger than it needs to be. Too fine and the thread frays and breaks. Too coarse and you leave visible holes and a weak seam.

## Thread to needle

Published as ranges, because that is how the thread makers give them. Within a range, go finer for a tight weave and coarser for something dense or layered.

**Be careful with single-figure charts.** A lot of tables circulating online give one needle per thread size. They are a simplification, and at the coarse end the simplification goes wrong: T270 is commonly listed against a 160/23, where the thread makers publish 180/24 to 220/26. A 160/23 with T270 will chafe the thread in the eye and break it. When a chart and the thread maker disagree, the thread maker is right.

## Reading needle sizes

Industrial needles carry two numbers, such as **110/18**. The first is metric — the blade diameter in hundredths of a millimetre, so 110 means 1.10 mm. The second is the American size for the same needle. They are two names for one thing, not two measurements.

## Needle systems

The system is the shank and length, and it has nothing to do with size. A needle of the right size in the wrong system will not fit, or will not time correctly.

- **135×16** — leather point, the usual choice for walking foot machines on leather and vinyl

- **135×17** — round point, the same system for woven cloth

- **134** (DPx5) — many flat-bed lockstitch machines

- **190** — some cylinder-arm and heavier machines

- **130/705H** (HAx1) — domestic machines



Your machine’s manual states its system. If you do not have the manual, the system is usually stamped on the needle already in it.

## Round point or leather point

This matters more than most people expect.

A **round point** (R) pushes between the yarns of a woven cloth without cutting them. Use it on anything woven. Put a leather point through woven cloth and you cut the yarns, which weakens the seam along its whole length.

A **leather point** is a cutting point, ground to slice a small clean incision rather than punch a hole. The grind determines which way the cut lies and therefore how the stitches sit — LR is the common general-purpose leather point and gives a neat line slightly angled to the seam.

**Vinyl is the awkward one.** It behaves like leather to the needle but is usually backed with a woven scrim. A leather point cuts that backing. Many trimmers use a round point on vinyl for that reason — worth testing on an offcut before committing on a customer’s job.

## Change needles more often than you think

A needle blunts long before it looks blunt. A blunted point pushes material down rather than piercing it, which is a common cause of skipped stitches and puckering that gets blamed on tension. If a machine that was sewing well starts misbehaving, put a new needle in before you touch anything else. It is the cheapest thing in the workshop and the most frequent culprit.
