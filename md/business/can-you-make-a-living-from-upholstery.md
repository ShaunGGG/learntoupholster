# Can you make a living from upholstery?

*Business Hub · Starting Out*

> Yes, but almost certainly not in the way you are picturing. Very few people make a living from one kind of upholstery.

Canonical: https://www.learntoupholster.com/business/can-you-make-a-living-from-upholstery

## Is upholstery a viable career?

Yes, but almost certainly not in the way you are picturing. Very few people make a living from one kind of upholstery. Thirty-four years in, my own workshop rests on four separate trainings — traditional work, modern methods, vehicle trimming and antique restoration — and the money comes from whichever of those is busy that month. Right now a good share of it is campervan conversion work, a market that did not exist when I started. The trade pays. It pays people who keep adding to what they can do.

## The short answer

Yes. You can make a living from upholstery.

The longer answer is that most people asking the question have a picture in their head of what that living looks like, and the picture is usually wrong. They imagine a workshop full of beautiful antique frames, hand-stitched edges, and customers who understand what that costs.

That work exists. I do it, and it is the work I love. It is not what pays for the building.

## How I actually got here

I am going to lay this out properly, because "yes you can" is worthless without knowing what it took.

**Trained in traditional upholstery, using modern methods.** That was thirty-four years ago, and I stayed with that firm for over ten years. Ten years at somebody else's bench, being paid to learn, making the mistakes on their time rather than my own.

**Then vehicle trimming**, for five years. Car seat reupholstery, carpet trimming, headliner trimming. A completely different discipline with different materials, different machines and different tolerances.

**Then self-employed**, where I landed a job restoring antique furniture and was taught proper traditional methods — the hand skills that the first job had only partly given me.

**Then my own business**, with my wife handling the administration and the books, built on everything the previous three had put in.

Four separate trainings, more than three decades. Nobody handed me a career. Each stage added a skill set, and the business I run now leans on all four of them.

## "Modern methods" is a moving target

Here is something worth understanding early, because it changes how you think about learning the trade.

I was trained in traditional upholstery using modern methods. Thirty-four years later, what counted as modern then is not modern now. The foams have changed. The adhesives have changed. Fire regulations have changed more than once. Staples, machines, fabrics, interlinings — all different.

**The trade you enter is not the trade you retire from.**

Which means the thing worth acquiring is not a fixed body of knowledge that you learn once and own forever. It is the habit of still learning after you already know how. The upholsterers who struggle are usually not the ones who know least. They are the ones who stopped adding.

## Nobody knows all of it

I have thirty-four years at the bench, an AMUSF accreditation and a book with my name on it, and I still learn something new most weeks.

That is not modesty for the sake of it. It is how the trade works. Every upholsterer develops their own style, their own order of doing things, their own small solutions to recurring problems. Watch two experienced people cover the same chair and you will see two different routes to a good result.

**There are very few fixed rules in upholstery.** There are principles that hold — support before comfort, shape before cover, do not build on a frame that is not sound — but the techniques on top of them vary enormously, by region, by training, by era, by workshop.

Be wary of anyone who tells you there is only one right way to do something. Usually what they mean is that there is only one way they know.

This matters for a beginner because it takes the pressure off. You are not trying to find the single correct method. You are assembling a way of working that suits your hands, your workshop and the pieces in front of you.

## So where does the money actually come from?

This is the part nobody answers honestly, so here is my own business as an example.

**Vehicle trimming, especially campervan conversions.** This is a genuinely good part of the business now, and it is worth its own paragraph below. Campervan conversion barely existed as a market when I trained.

**Maintenance work for holiday parks.** Fast repetitive repair work, monthly visits, predictable. It is not craft, and it pays the overheads and smooths the year out. See [caravan and holiday park work](https://www.learntoupholster.com/business/caravan-and-holiday-park-work).

**Commercial and contract jobs**, which come in occasionally and are worth having when the specification is right — and worth turning down when it is not, as in [the 76-chair contract I turned down](https://www.learntoupholster.com/business/the-contract-i-turned-down).

**Domestic reupholstery**, the bread and butter, and where most people start.

**Teaching, writing and the website.** A later addition, and only possible because of the decades that came before it.

**Traditional and antique restoration** — the work I most want to do, funded by everything above.

Six income streams. Not one of them would support the business on its own.

## Campervan conversion is where I would look right now

If you are starting today and want to know where the growth is, this is my honest answer.

Campervan conversion has become a real industry, and it is full of small converters who are good at joinery, electrics and plumbing and have no upholstery capability at all. They need cushions, seat bases and backs, bed boards, upholstered panels, headlining. Every single van.

I work with local converters doing exactly that, and it has three qualities that make it better than chasing domestic customers:

- **It repeats.** A converter building twenty vans a year needs twenty sets of cushions. Win the relationship once and the work keeps arriving.

- **They are easy to find and easy to approach.** They are small local businesses with a workshop and a phone number, not a procurement department.

- **You are not competing on price with a sofa.** The customer has already committed forty thousand pounds to the conversion. The cushions are not the thing they are trying to save money on.



It also uses vehicle trimming skills that most furniture upholsterers do not have, which keeps the competition thin.

If I were starting again now, I would learn foam work and machine skills properly and go and talk to every van converter within an hour's drive.

## The pattern worth taking from all this

Look back at that list of income streams and notice what they have in common: they draw on four different trainings, and they get busy at different times.

Domestic work dies in January. Park maintenance carries the season and the shutdown refurbishment lands in the dead months. Campervan work follows the conversion market. Contract jobs arrive unpredictably. Teaching fits around all of it.

**Breadth is not indecision. It is what makes the year survivable.**

The upholsterer who can only do one kind of work is at the mercy of whether that one kind of work is busy. The one who can do four has something to fall back on every month of the year.

## What I would tell someone starting out

1. **Get paid to learn if you possibly can.** Ten years at someone else's bench taught me more, faster, and at no financial risk, than ten years of struggling alone would have.

2. **Add a second discipline early.** Vehicle trimming, soft furnishings, loose covers, foam work. The second skill is worth more than being slightly better at the first.

3. **Work out your numbers before you set a price.** Most workshops that fail are busy when they fail. [How much should an upholsterer charge](https://www.learntoupholster.com/business/how-much-should-an-upholsterer-charge) and [why being busy is not the same as making money](https://www.learntoupholster.com/business/busy-but-not-making-money) are the two things I wish someone had made me read at twenty-five.

4. **Do not wait to feel ready.** You will still be learning in thirty years. That is the job, not a stage you pass through.

5. **Expect the first two years to be hard.** Word of mouth is the engine of this trade and it takes eighteen months to two years to start turning. That is not failure, it is the timescale.



## The honest summary

Can you make a living from upholstery? Yes. I have, my wife and I both do, and it has supported a family.

Can you make a living doing nothing but hand-stitched traditional work, from day one, in most parts of the country? Almost certainly not. That work is a privilege you build towards and fund with everything else.

The trade pays. It pays people who keep adding to what they can do, who know which of their skills is actually making the money, and who are still curious after thirty years.

That last one matters more than it sounds.
