## Does running an upholstery business with your spouse actually work?

It can, and ours does, but the arrangement matters more than the goodwill. The single best decision we made was separate spaces — my wife has her office, I have the workshop, and either of us can disappear when the other is getting on their nerves. She handles the administration and the books and is qualified to do it, which makes her a partner rather than someone roped in to help. Most upholstery workshops fail on the admin rather than the craft, so that division is not a nicety. It is the business.

## Separate spaces

If you take one practical thing from this article, take this one.

My wife has her own office. I have the workshop. They are not the same room, and that turns out to matter enormously.

If I am getting on her nerves, she can shut a door. If she is getting on mine, I can go back to the bench. Neither of us has to have the conversation about it, and by lunchtime it has usually stopped being a thing at all.

Couples who work in the same room, all day, every day, are asking a great deal of the relationship. Two rooms costs almost nothing and removes most of the friction before it starts. If you are setting this up, plan the separation in from the beginning rather than discovering you need it eighteen months later.

## What each of us actually does

She runs the administration and the bookkeeping. I am at the bench.

That is not a convenient split — it is a split by capability. She holds business management qualifications. She is doing the part of the business she is trained for and I am not.

There is a real difference between a partner and a spouse who has been roped in to help with the paperwork. One is a person doing a job they are good at, with authority over it. The other is someone doing a chore under supervision, badly, resentfully, and probably at the kitchen table on a Sunday.

If the arrangement you are contemplating is the second one, think again. That is not a business structure, it is a slow argument.

## Most workshops fail on the admin, not the craft

This is the structural argument for the whole thing, and it is worth stating plainly.

Very few upholstery businesses go under because the upholstery was not good enough. They go under because the quoting was wrong, the money was not chased, the tax was not set aside, the books were three months behind, or nobody worked out that being busy was not the same as making money.

Every one of those is an administrative failure, and every one of them is the thing a skilled bench upholsterer is least likely to be good at or interested in.

So having somebody whose actual job is that side of it is not a luxury bolted onto a craft business. In a lot of cases it *is* the business, and the craft is the part that would have survived anyway.

## The invisible half

Something worth saying if you are the one at the bench.

Bench work produces evidence. At the end of a day there is a chair, and you can photograph it and feel it was a day's work.

Admin produces nothing you can point at. A day of chasing an invoice, filing certificates, doing the VAT and answering fifteen enquiries leaves no object behind. It is completely invisible, and invisible work is very easy to undervalue — including, if you are not careful, by the person doing the visible half.

Notice it. Say so occasionally. It costs nothing and it is true.

## The honest risks

I am not going to pretend this is all upside.

**Every penny comes from one place.** Two people, one business, one income. There is no partner in a steady job to carry a bad quarter. That concentration is the single biggest financial risk of the arrangement and it is worth being clear-eyed about — it is the same reason I would not let one client become most of my turnover.

**You never fully switch off.** Your business partner is also the person you have dinner with. Work follows you home because home is where the other half of the business is. We are better at this than we were, but it takes deliberate effort, and "we are not talking about the workshop tonight" has to be a sentence either of you can say.

**Disagreements have nowhere to go.** In a normal job you have a row at work and go home to complain about it. Here there is no second location and no neutral party. Which is another argument for separate rooms, and for settling things rather than letting them run.

**Holidays close the business.** When both of you are away, everything is away — the bench and the office. Plan for that rather than being surprised by it.

## What makes it work

Four things, in the order I would rank them.

1. **Separate spaces.** Covered above, and it is genuinely first.

2. **Clear roles with real authority.** She decides the admin. I decide the bench. Neither of us second-guesses the other in their own area, which removes most of what couples in business actually fall out about.

3. **Pay yourselves properly, both of you.** Two people working means two wages, not one wage and a helper. How you structure that has tax implications and they differ by country — that is a conversation for an accountant, not for me, but the principle is that the invisible half is paid for the work.

4. **Decide when work stops.** Not perfectly, but decide.



## The thing I did not expect

Working with my wife is the reason I can still do this job.

That sounds like sentiment and it is not. In [the physical toll](https://www.learntoupholster.com/business/the-physical-toll) I wrote about what piecework did to my hands, and about how the pain returns if my speed creeps up on a batch of identical chairs. The reason it mostly does not return now is that nobody is counting my units. I can break a batch up, do something else for a day, and come back to it.

That is only possible because of how the business is set up and who is running the other half of it. Being able to work at my own pace is not a personality trait. It is a structural feature of a business run by two people who are not trying to squeeze each other.

## Teaching

The other unexpected part is how much teaching there is in it.

I have taught my wife a great deal about the trade, because you cannot quote, order or explain a job to a customer without understanding what actually happens at the bench. And I get to teach my children a craft.

That last one matters more to me than it might have done twenty years ago. We are raising them in a world where a great many jobs are being automated, and I do not know which of them will still exist by the time they are choosing. What I do know is that a pair of hands that can strip a chair, judge a frame, stitch an edge and put it back together properly is not a skill that disappears — it is thirty years of judgement that has to be built by doing, and it cannot be downloaded.

Giving a child a trade is one of the more durable things you can hand them.

## Would I recommend it

If the roles are genuinely complementary, if both people are qualified for their half, and if you have two rooms — yes. It is challenging and rewarding in roughly equal measure, and the business is far stronger than either of us would manage alone.

If it would mean one of you doing a job you do not want, are not trained for, and have no authority over, then no. Hire a bookkeeper instead. It is cheaper than the alternative.
