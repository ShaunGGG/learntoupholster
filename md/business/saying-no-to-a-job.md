## How do I turn down work without damaging my reputation?

Decline quickly, politely, and without a detailed justification. "I'm afraid it's not something I can take on" is a complete sentence and needs no defence. Do not price yourself out instead of refusing, because sometimes people say yes. Do not go quiet and hope they lose interest, because that is what makes people leave reviews. If you can name someone better suited, do — a good referral turns a refusal into a favour.

## Why this is hard

Turning work away feels wrong, especially early on. There is always the thought that the diary might be empty in three months and you will regret it.

But the jobs you do not want are rarely just unappealing. They are usually unprofitable, awkward, or attached to somebody who is going to be difficult, and those three tend to travel together. Taking them costs you the capacity to do work you actually want.

## The mistake almost everyone makes first

Quoting a silly number to make them go away.

It fails in both directions. Sometimes they say yes, and now you are committed to the job you did not want, at a price that has made the customer's expectations enormous. And sometimes they tell people you quoted an outrageous figure, which is a worse outcome than simply having said no.

If you do not want the work, refuse it. Do not price it.

## How to actually say it

Short, warm, prompt, no reasons.

> "Thanks for thinking of me. I'm afraid it's not something I can take on at the moment — I hope you find someone who can help."

That is enough. The urge to explain is strong and should be resisted, because every reason you give is something the customer can argue with. "I'm too busy" invites *when are you free?* "It's not really my sort of work" invites *but could you have a look anyway?*

No reason, no argument.

## Refer if you can

The single best thing you can do is point them somewhere useful.

> "It's not something I take on, but [name] does a lot of that sort of work and is worth a call."

You have now solved their problem without doing the job. People remember that, and it is how referrals come back to you later. If you cannot think of anyone, the [find an upholsterer](https://www.learntoupholster.com/find-an-upholsterer) page is a reasonable thing to point at.

## The ones worth declining

Patterns that come up repeatedly:

- Furniture with no structural future — chipboard frames, stapled softwood, anything not worth the labour

- A customer who has already fallen out with another upholsterer

- Fabric you have advised against and they insist on anyway

- Work you have not done before, at a price that leaves no room to learn on it

- Anything where the person is haggling before you have even quoted



That last one is the clearest signal in the trade. Someone negotiating before they know the price is telling you what the whole job will be like.

## Say it early

The cost of declining rises every day you leave it. A refusal on the first phone call is a minor disappointment. A refusal after two weeks of silence, three chasing emails and a site visit is a grievance.
