# Why does velvet go patchy?

*Blog · Fabrics*

> Velvet does not go patchy — it shades. Why pile fabric changes colour as it is pressed, which velvets recover and which mark permanently, and what pile direction does to your fabric quantity.

Canonical: https://www.learntoupholster.com/blog/why-does-velvet-go-patchy

## Why does my velvet sofa look patchy?

Because velvet is a pile fabric, and pile reflects light differently depending on which way it is lying. Press it, sit on it, or stroke it the wrong way and that patch reflects light differently to the cloth around it, so it reads as a lighter or darker mark. It is called shading or pressure marking, it is inherent to the way velvet is made, and it is not a fault. Whether it comes out again depends almost entirely on the fibre: cotton and polyester velvets usually recover with steam and brushing, while viscose velvet very often does not. If a supplier tells you their velvet will never mark, they are either selling polyester or not telling the truth.

Velvet is the fabric that generates more complaints than any other, and almost none of them are faults. It is also the fabric people fall in love with hardest, which is why the disappointment stings when the first visitor gets up and leaves a shadow across the seat.

So it is worth explaining what is actually happening, because once you understand it you can either choose a velvet that suits how you live, or stop worrying about it.

## Velvet is a pile fabric, and that is the whole story

Most cloth is flat. The surface you see is the weave itself, and it looks the same from wherever you stand.

Velvet is not flat. It has a dense forest of short fibre ends standing up from a woven backing, and those ends behave like millions of tiny mirrors. When they all lean the same way, light bounces off them uniformly and you get that deep, even lustre. When a patch of them gets pushed over in a different direction, that patch throws light somewhere else and reads as a different shade.

Nothing has stained. Nothing has faded. Nothing has worn. The fibres are simply lying at a different angle to their neighbours.

Move round the room and the mark will often change or disappear entirely, which is the giveaway. A stain looks like a stain from every angle. Shading does not.

## The word velvet tells you almost nothing

This is where most of the trouble starts. Velvet describes a construction, not a material — and the fibre it is made from matters far more than the word on the label.

- Cotton velvet. Traditional, matt rather than glossy, handsome. Marks readily but usually recovers with steam and brushing. Good on period pieces.

- Linen velvet. Similar behaviour, with a drier, more textured handle. Creases as linen does.

- Mohair velvet. The connoisseur's choice and priced accordingly. Angora goat fibre — springy, resilient, extraordinarily hard-wearing, and it resists crushing better than anything else. Original Victorian and Edwardian pieces were often covered in it and a great many are still going.

- Silk velvet. Beautiful, fragile, and honestly not an upholstery cloth for anything that gets sat on daily.

- Viscose velvet. Often sold on its glorious shine and its price. It is regenerated cellulose, it loses a great deal of its strength when damp, and its pile does not spring back. This is the one that marks permanently. Also the one most often behind a furious phone call.

- Polyester velvet. Frequently sold as performance velvet. Far less glamorous to a purist, and by a wide margin the most forgiving in a family house. Resists crushing, tolerates cleaning, and recovers well.

Two near relatives worth knowing, because they get called velvet in shops. Velveteen has a much shorter pile made from the weft rather than a separate warp, so it is flatter and marks less. Velour is knitted rather than woven, which makes it stretchy — a different animal entirely, and it behaves differently under tension on a frame.

## Pile direction, and why your upholsterer keeps going on about it

Run your hand over velvet one way and it feels smooth. Run it the other way and it feels like it is resisting slightly, and the colour shifts. That is the pile direction, and deciding it is one of the first things done in the workshop before a single panel is cut.

The convention in this country is pile running down — smooth when you stroke from the top of the piece towards the floor, and from the back of a seat towards the front edge. Pile down gives a lighter, more lustrous look, sheds dust rather than collecting it, and wears more evenly because the pile is being pushed the way it already wants to lie.

Pile running up gives a deeper, richer, darker colour. It is a legitimate choice and some designers prefer it, particularly on a statement chair. It will show marks more and it will collect more dust.

What matters far more than which one you choose is that every panel runs the same way. One panel cut upside down on an otherwise perfect job will read as a different colour under the window, and there is no fixing it short of recutting. It is the single most common error on velvet in workshops that do not work with it often.

If you are buying velvet and having it made up elsewhere, ask outright which way the pile will run and whether every panel will match. A workshop that answers immediately and without hedging has done this before.

## Velvet costs you more fabric, before anyone has bought anything

Because every panel has to run the same way, velvet is a one-way cloth. On a plain fabric an upholsterer can turn a panel ninety degrees or flip it end to end to squeeze it out of an awkward gap in the cutting plan. On velvet none of that is available.

In practice this behaves much like a pattern repeat: expect to allow more cloth than a plain fabric of the same width for the same piece, and take the estimate from someone who knows it is velvet. Getting a quantity from a plain-fabric rule of thumb and then buying velvet is a reliable way to end up half a metre short with a discontinued dye lot.

## Pressure marks: what comes out and what does not

Three different things get lumped together under "marking", and they have different outcomes.

- Shading. Broad soft variations in tone across a panel, changing as you move around the room. Entirely normal, present from day one on most velvets, and part of the character. Nothing to fix.

- Pressure marks. Defined patches where somebody has sat, knelt or set something down. On cotton, linen, mohair and polyester these usually lift with gentle steam and brushing. On viscose they very often do not.

- Watermarking. Wavy, cloudy patterns that appear after the pile gets damp — sometimes from cleaning, sometimes just from a humid room or a wet coat laid over an arm. On viscose this is generally permanent. It is why you should never wet-clean a viscose velvet.

To lift an ordinary pressure mark: hold a steamer or steam iron above the surface without touching it, let the steam relax the pile, then brush gently in the pile direction with a soft clothes brush and let it dry undisturbed. Patience works better than pressure. Never press an iron onto velvet — it will flatten the pile permanently and that is a mark nothing removes.

## Living with it

None of this makes velvet a bad choice. It makes it a choice that rewards knowing what you are buying.

- Brush the piece occasionally with a soft brush, always in the pile direction.

- Vacuum with an upholstery brush head on low suction, again in the pile direction.

- Rotate and turn loose cushions so wear and marking spread evenly.

- Blot spills immediately, never rub — rubbing distorts the pile and leaves a mark worse than the spill.

- Keep viscose velvet away from anything damp, and get any real cleaning done by a specialist who has been told what fibre it is.

- Check the Martindale rating on the spec sheet, and do not put a light-domestic velvet on the sofa the whole family uses.

## What I tell people choosing velvet

I ask two questions. Who sits on it, and can you live with marks?

If the answer is a formal sitting room used twice a week and yes, then almost any velvet will make you happy, and a cotton or mohair one will look glorious for decades.

If the answer is two children, a dog and every evening, then I steer firmly towards a good polyester performance velvet. It will not have quite the depth of a cotton velvet under a lamp. It will also still look like a sofa in five years, which matters more.

And if somebody has fallen in love with a viscose velvet, I tell them plainly what it will do, put it in writing on the quote, and then cover the piece in it if they still want it. It is their sofa. What is not acceptable is them finding out afterwards.

## Common questions

### Are pressure marks in velvet a fault?

No. Shading and pressure marking are inherent to pile fabric and are specifically excluded from most fabric warranties. The pile fibres are lying at a different angle and reflecting light differently, which is how velvet works. A genuine fault would be something like a pile that pulls out, an uneven backing, or a flaw running through the weave.

### Can pressure marks be removed from velvet?

Usually on cotton, linen, mohair and polyester velvet. Hold a steamer above the surface without touching it, brush gently in the pile direction with a soft brush, and let it dry undisturbed. Viscose velvet very often will not recover, and any watermarking on viscose is generally permanent, which is why it should never be wet-cleaned.

### Which velvet is the most hard-wearing?

Mohair velvet, comfortably, and it is priced to match — Victorian and Edwardian pieces covered in it are frequently still in service. For an everyday budget, a good polyester performance velvet is the practical answer: it resists crushing, recovers from marks and tolerates cleaning. Check the Martindale rating rather than trusting the description.

### Does velvet need more fabric than plain cloth?

Yes. Because the pile must run the same way on every panel, velvet is a one-way cloth and panels cannot be turned or flipped to fit the cutting plan efficiently. Allow extra in much the same way you would for a pattern repeat, and always tell whoever is estimating that the fabric is velvet before the order goes in.
