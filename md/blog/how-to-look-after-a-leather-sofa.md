# How to look after a leather sofa

*Blog · Leather & Vinyl*

> Which finish you have decides everything, and there is a two-minute test that tells you. The routine that keeps leather alive for decades, and the everyday products that quietly destroy it.

Canonical: https://www.learntoupholster.com/blog/how-to-look-after-a-leather-sofa

## How do you care for a leather sofa?

Keep it out of direct sunlight and away from radiators, dust it weekly, wipe it occasionally with a barely damp cloth and dry it straight away, condition it once or twice a year with a product matched to its finish, and blot spills immediately rather than rubbing them. Never use baby wipes, household cleaning sprays, solvents, silicone polish or olive oil on it. Everything else depends on which finish you have — pigmented, semi-aniline or aniline — and a drop of water will tell you that in two minutes.

Leather is the easiest upholstery material to look after and the one most often ruined by people trying to look after it. Almost all of the damage I see was caused by cleaning rather than by use.

The good news is that the actual routine is very small. The important part is knowing what you are working with first.

## Find out what finish you have

This decides every other decision, and it takes two minutes. Find somewhere inconspicuous — the back, or under a cushion — and put a single drop of water on it.

- The drop sits on the surface as a bead. Pigmented — also called protected or corrected-grain. Much the most common upholstery leather. It has a pigment layer and a topcoat over it, so the colour is uniform, natural markings are hidden, and the surface feels smooth and slightly cool. It is the most robust and the easiest to look after.

- The drop soaks in within a few seconds and darkens the leather. Aniline — dyed right through with no protective coating. Natural scars and grain variation show. It feels warm and soft, it develops the best patina of any leather, and it stains if you look at it wrongly.

- The drop sits briefly, then slowly darkens. Semi-aniline — dyed through with a light protective coat. Middle ground in every sense.

- Velvety, matt, marks when you brush your hand across it. Nubuck or suede. Different material entirely. Never use liquid on it; it wants a specialist brush and a specialist cleaner.

Wipe the drop off afterwards either way. And if the surface is flaking or lifting anywhere, stop — you may not have leather at all, which is covered in why leather peels.

## The routine, which is smaller than you think

Weekly: dust it. That is genuinely most of the job. Household dust is abrasive, and on a surface that gets sat on it works like very fine sandpaper against the finish. A dry microfibre cloth is enough.

Monthly: vacuum the crevices and the cushion joins with a soft brush attachment. Never a hard nozzle straight onto the leather — the plastic edge scratches finishes.

Occasionally: wipe over with a cloth wrung out in clean water so it is barely damp, then dry immediately with a second cloth. Not wet. Not soaked. On aniline, be even more sparing, because water alone can leave a ring.

Twice a year, or once if the house is not heavily heated: condition it.

## Conditioning, and what it actually does

People talk about feeding leather as though it were hungry. What conditioners really do is replace the oils and plasticisers that keep the fibre structure flexible, and maintain the finish sitting on top. Leather that dries out loses flexibility, and leather that has lost flexibility cracks when it flexes — which it does every time somebody sits down.

Practical points:

- Match the product to the finish. A conditioner for pigmented leather is not the right thing for aniline, and an aniline product can darken or blotch a protected leather. Read the label, and if it does not say, do not buy it.

- Test on a hidden area first and leave it a few hours before doing the whole piece.

- Little and often beats a lot occasionally. A thin application, worked in with a soft cloth, excess buffed off. Leather cannot absorb a thick coat, so the surplus just sits there attracting dust.

- More often in a dry, heated house or if the sofa lives near a radiator. Central heating is what dries leather out, not use.

## The things that quietly destroy it

This list matters more than everything above, because these do damage that cannot be undone.

- Baby wipes. The great destroyer, and the one I see most. They contain surfactants and often alcohol, they strip the finish a little at a time, and people use them precisely because they seem gentle. By the time the surface starts looking patchy the finish has already gone.

- Household cleaning sprays and antibacterial wipes. Same problem, faster.

- Solvents, alcohol, nail polish remover, white spirit. Dissolve the finish on contact.

- Silicone furniture polish. Builds a layer that looks glossy and then cannot be removed, and a leather with silicone on it cannot be properly recoloured later. It closes the door on the repair you will eventually want.

- Olive oil and other kitchen remedies. They go rancid, they darken unevenly, and they never come out.

- Saddle soap. Made for unfinished tack leather, too harsh for finished upholstery leather.

- Direct sunlight. The single biggest cause of fading and drying, and entirely preventable. If you can see where the sun falls across the back cushions, move the sofa or close the curtains.

- Radiators and log burners. A sofa with one end against a heat source will age visibly faster at that end.

If you take one thing from this article: stop using baby wipes on leather. More sofas are ruined by them than by children, dogs and spills combined.

## Spills

Blot, never rub. Rubbing pushes liquid into the grain and, on aniline, spreads the stain outwards into a bigger and more visible ring.

- Water and most drinks: blot immediately with a dry cloth, then let it dry naturally away from heat. Do not aim a hairdryer at it.

- Grease, butter, food oils: blot, then sprinkle talc or cornflour over the mark and leave it several hours to draw the oil out. Brush off gently. Repeat rather than scrubbing.

- Ink: leave it alone and ring a specialist. Every home remedy for ink on leather — hairspray especially — works by dissolving the finish, which means you trade a small ink mark for a permanent bare patch.

- Pet accidents: blot, clean gently with a leather-appropriate cleaner, and get to it quickly. Urine damages leather chemically as well as leaving a smell.

## Two problems people do not expect

Denim dye transfer. Dark jeans on a pale sofa transfer indigo into the leather, particularly on aniline and semi-aniline where there is little or no protective coat. It builds up gradually and is very difficult to remove once it has been there a while. If you have a light-coloured leather, deal with it early and often rather than waiting.

Scratches. On aniline and semi-aniline, light scratches often disappear if you warm them with a fingertip and buff gently with a soft cloth — the leather is dyed through, so there is colour underneath. On pigmented leather a scratch that has gone through the finish shows a different colour underneath and will not buff out; that is a job for a leather refinisher, who can fill and recolour it.

## Habits that add years

- Rotate and turn loose cushions so wear spreads evenly.

- Do not sit on the arms — arms are the least supported part of the frame and the first leather to stretch.

- Keep pets' claws trimmed if they are on the sofa, which they are.

- Keep it a foot clear of walls and radiators so air circulates behind it.

## When to call somebody

Leather refinishing is a proper trade in its own right, and a good refinisher can clean back, fill, recolour and reseal a tired pigmented leather to a standard that surprises people. That is very often the right answer for a sofa that looks past it but has a sound frame — considerably cheaper than recovering, and it keeps the original leather.

If the leather itself has split or torn through, that becomes an upholstery job: panel replacement rather than refinishing.

## What good looks like

The last thing worth saying is that leather is meant to change. A hide that has softened, darkened slightly and taken on the shape of the people who use it is not damaged — that is the whole point of buying leather rather than something that stays identical until it fails.

Dust it, keep it out of the sun, condition it twice a year, and keep the baby wipes in the kitchen. That is the entire job, and it will still look good in twenty years.

## Common questions

### How often should you condition a leather sofa?

Once or twice a year for most homes, more often if the house is dry or heavily heated, or if the sofa sits near a radiator. Use a conditioner matched to the finish — a pigmented-leather product on aniline, or the reverse, can darken or blotch it. Apply thinly, work it in with a soft cloth and buff off the excess, because leather cannot absorb a thick coat.

### Can you use baby wipes on a leather sofa?

No. They are the most common cause of avoidable damage to leather upholstery. The surfactants, and often alcohol, strip the protective finish a small amount at a time, and by the point the surface starts looking patchy or dull the finish has already gone. Use a barely damp cloth and dry immediately, or a cleaner made for leather.

### How do you clean a leather sofa at home?

Dust it weekly with a dry microfibre cloth, vacuum the crevices monthly with a soft brush attachment rather than a hard nozzle, and wipe over occasionally with a cloth wrung out in clean water so it is barely damp, drying it straight away with a second cloth. Blot spills rather than rubbing. Avoid household sprays, solvents, silicone polish and kitchen remedies entirely.

### Can scratches be removed from leather?

On aniline and semi-aniline leather, light scratches often fade if you warm them with a fingertip and buff gently with a soft cloth, because the hide is dyed all the way through. On pigmented leather a scratch that has broken the surface finish reveals a different colour beneath and will not buff out — that needs a leather refinisher to fill and recolour, which is a routine job for them.
