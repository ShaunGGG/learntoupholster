# Upholstery thread guide — sizes, nylon vs polyester

*Sewing · Thread*

> Which upholstery thread for which job: bonded nylon or polyester, Tex sizes, commercial and government numbering, and needle pairing.

Canonical: https://www.learntoupholster.com/sewing-thread

Which thread for which job, what the three numbering systems mean, and the one property that decides nylon or polyester.

**If you read nothing else:** use bonded **polyester** for anything that will see sunlight — marine, motorcycle, garden, campervan, caravan — and bonded **nylon** for indoor work and vehicle interiors. **T70** is the everyday upholstery size. Most people who get this wrong put nylon somewhere sunny.

## The three numbering systems

The same thread is sold under three sets of numbers, which is most of why this is confusing. **Commercial** sizes (#69, #92) are the American trade standard. **Tex** is the international one and is the weight in grams of 1,000 metres — so a higher number is a thicker thread. **Government** sizes (E, F, FF) appear on a lot of US-made thread and are a legacy of military specification.

They line up like this. Needle sizes are given as ranges because that is how the thread makers publish them.

T70 is highlighted because it is the size you will use most, and the heaviest a domestic machine will generally manage.

## Nylon or polyester

Both are bonded, both are strong, and for most indoor work either will do. The difference that actually decides it is ultraviolet light.

### Bonded nylon

Best for: Indoor work, vehicle interiors, leather

In its favour

- Highest tensile strength for its size

- Excellent abrasion resistance — suits seams that get slid across

- More elastic, so it gives rather than snapping under a sudden load

- Widest colour range in most suppliers’ stock



Against it

- Degrades faster in ultraviolet light

- Absorbs a little moisture, which matters in damp conditions



### Bonded polyester

Best for: Anything that sees sunlight

In its favour

- Far better UV resistance — the deciding property outdoors

- Low moisture absorption

- Resists most cleaning chemicals well

- Nearly as strong as nylon at the same size



Against it

- Slightly less abrasion resistant than nylon

- Slightly less elastic



**The mistake worth avoiding.** Nylon is the stronger, more abrasion-resistant thread, so it feels like the better choice — and people reach for it out of habit. Put it in a boat, a motorcycle seat or a campervan and the sun will have it long before the seam wears out. In sunlight, polyester wins on the only property that matters there.

## What is bonded thread?

A resin coating applied after twisting. It binds the plies together so the thread resists untwisting as it runs through the machine, reduces friction at the needle, and stops the end fraying when you thread up. For any upholstery work at speed, bonded is what you want — unbonded thread of the same size will fray and snarl.

## S twist and Z twist — and why the trade contradicts itself

Plied thread is twisted in one of two directions, described as **S** or **Z**. Sewing machines are built around one of them, and using the wrong one makes the thread untwist as it runs, which shows up as fraying, snarling and broken thread that no amount of tension adjustment will fix.

**Machine sewing thread should have a final Z twist.** That is true for domestic, industrial and longarm machines alike, and for every major brand. S twist is for hand sewing, and for the outside needle of certain twin-needle machines built for it.

**Ignore “left twist” and “right twist”.** The trade uses those terms inconsistently. One large supplier’s thread guide calls Z twist the left twist while their own blog describes S twist as clockwise; another supplier reverses the pair entirely. The S and Z labels are unambiguous and the left/right ones are not, so buy on S or Z and disregard the rest.

## Can a domestic machine sew upholstery thread?

Up to **T70**, usually yes — that is the recognised ceiling for domestic machines, and it is enough for a great deal of furniture work. Above it you need an industrial machine: T90 and up want a larger needle, a bigger hook and more thread path than a domestic head is built for. Forcing it produces skipped stitches and, eventually, a timing problem.
