# Upholstery thread guide

*Sewing · Thread*

> Which upholstery thread for which job: bonded nylon or polyester, Tex sizes, commercial and government numbering, and needle pairing.

Canonical: https://www.learntoupholster.com/sewing-thread

Which thread for which job, what the three numbering systems mean, and the one property that decides nylon or polyester.

If you read nothing else: use bonded polyester for anything that will see sunlight — marine, motorcycle, garden, campervan, caravan — and bonded nylon for indoor work and vehicle interiors. T70 is the everyday upholstery size. Most people who get this wrong put nylon somewhere sunny.

## Four numbering systems, one thread

The same cone is sold under four sets of numbers depending on who made it and where you are, which is most of why this is confusing.

- Tex — the international standard, and the one worth learning. It is the weight in grams of 1,000 metres, so a higher number is a thicker thread. T70 weighs 70 g per kilometre.
- Commercial size (#69, #92) — the North American trade standard, derived from denier. Common worldwide because so much bonded thread is made to it.
- Ticket number (Tkt 40, Tkt 20) — what is printed on Coats and Gütermann cones, so it is what a great many upholsterers outside North America actually buy by. It runs backwards: a higher ticket number is a finer thread.
- Government size (E, F, FF) — a US military legacy, still printed on a good deal of thread wherever it is sold.
They line up like this. The needle column gives the size most people reach for; there is a workable range either side of it, set out on the [needle page](https://www.learntoupholster.com/sewing-needles).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T35 | #33 | Tkt 80 | A | 90/14 | Very light work, linings, fine detail. Rare in upholstery. |
| T45 | #46 | Tkt 70 | B | 100/16 | Light upholstery, thin leather, cushion interiors, delicate work. |
| T70 | #69 | Tkt 40 | E | 110/18 | The standard upholstery thread. Sofas, chairs, most seams. The heaviest most domestic machines will manage. |

T70 is highlighted because it is the size you will use most, and the heaviest a domestic machine will generally manage. Ticket equivalents are the nearest standard ticket to (1,000 ÷ Tex) × 3, so treat them as close rather than exact.

Why #69 and T70 are nearly but not quite the same number. Both are calculated from denier, the weight in grams of 9,000 metres, but by different divisors. A bonded nylon of three 210-denier plies is 630 denier: divide by 9 and you get Tex 70; multiply by 0.11 and you get commercial size 69. The same thread, two sums. Polyester at the same ticket uses 220-denier plies, which works out at Tex 73 — and is still sold as T70, because Tex sizes are bracketed into fixed steps and anything between them rounds to the nearest. That is why you never see a T73.

## Nylon or polyester

Both are bonded, both are strong, and for most indoor work either will do. The difference that actually decides it is ultraviolet light.

### Bonded nylon

Best for: Indoor work, vehicle interiors, leather

In its favour

- Highest tensile strength for its size
- Excellent abrasion resistance — suits seams that get slid across
- More elastic, so it gives rather than snapping under a sudden load
- Widest colour range in most suppliers’ stock
Against it

- Degrades faster in ultraviolet light
- Absorbs a little moisture, which matters in damp conditions
### Bonded polyester

Best for: Anything that sees sunlight

In its favour

- Far better UV resistance — the deciding property outdoors
- Low moisture absorption
- Resists most cleaning chemicals well
- Nearly as strong as nylon at the same size
Against it

- Slightly less abrasion resistant than nylon
- Slightly less elastic
The mistake worth avoiding. Nylon is the stronger, more abrasion-resistant thread, so it feels like the better choice — and people reach for it out of habit. Put it in a boat, a motorcycle seat or a campervan and the sun will have it long before the seam wears out. In sunlight, polyester wins on the only property that matters there.

## What is bonded thread?

A resin coating applied after twisting. It binds the plies together so the thread resists untwisting as it runs through the machine, reduces friction at the needle, and stops the end fraying when you thread up. For any upholstery work at speed, bonded is what you want — unbonded thread of the same size will fray and snarl.

## Which thread for what

| Job | Thread | Size | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sofas and armchairs | Bonded nylon or polyester | T70 | The everyday upholstery size. Strong enough for any domestic seam without the stitch becoming a feature. |
| Dining chairs, cushions, light work | Bonded nylon or polyester | T45 – T70 | T45 where the seam is short and the cloth light; T70 if in doubt. |
| Car and van seats | Bonded nylon | T70 – T90 | Around the sizes vehicle manufacturers use — strong, and the stitch stays discreet. Nylon’s abrasion resistance suits a seat that is slid across daily. |
| Decorative top stitching on leather | Bonded nylon or polyester | T135 | Bold enough to read as a deliberate line rather than a seam. |
| Vinyl seating | Bonded polyester | T90 | Vinyl is usually a hard-use or wipe-clean situation. Polyester copes better with light and cleaning products. |
| Leather furniture | Bonded nylon or polyester | T90 | T135 where you want the stitching seen. |
| Marine and boat upholstery | Bonded polyester | T90 – T135 | Polyester, always. Sunlight is the thing that kills a marine seam, and nylon degrades in UV faster than polyester. |
| Motorcycle seats | Bonded polyester | T90 – T135 | Outdoors and uncovered most of its life. Same reasoning as marine. |
| Garden and outdoor furniture | Bonded polyester | T70 – T90 | Polyester for the UV. This is the one choice people get wrong most often. |
| Campervan interiors | Bonded polyester | T70 – T90 | Behind glass, in the sun, for years. A van interior gets far more light than a sofa in a front room. |
| Caravan and holiday-home seating | Bonded polyester | T70 | Hard use, strong light, and cleaned often. |

## S twist and Z twist — and why the trade contradicts itself

Plied thread is twisted in one of two directions, described as S or Z. Sewing machines are built around one of them, and using the wrong one makes the thread untwist as it runs, which shows up as fraying, snarling and broken thread that no amount of tension adjustment will fix.

Machine sewing thread should have a final Z twist. That is true for domestic, industrial and longarm machines alike, and for every major brand. S twist is for hand sewing, and for the outside needle of certain twin-needle machines built for it.

Ignore “left twist” and “right twist”. The trade uses those terms inconsistently. One large supplier’s thread guide calls Z twist the left twist while their own blog describes S twist as clockwise; another supplier reverses the pair entirely. The S and Z labels are unambiguous and the left/right ones are not, so buy on S or Z and disregard the rest.

## Can a domestic machine sew upholstery thread?

Up to T70, usually yes — that is the recognised ceiling for domestic machines, and it is enough for a great deal of furniture work. Above it you need an industrial machine: T90 and up want a larger needle, a bigger hook and more thread path than a domestic head is built for. Forcing it produces skipped stitches and, eventually, a timing problem.
