title: Explaining why upholstery costs what it does
question: How do I justify the price when a customer can buy a new sofa for less?
answer: Do not justify it — explain what they are buying. A new sofa at the same price is a
  different product: a stapled softwood frame with a five-year life, against a hardwood frame
  rebuilt to last another thirty. Talk about the frame, the hours and what happens underneath,
  not about how hard you work. And show them rather than telling them: a photograph of their
  own chair in the fabric they are considering ends more hesitation than any amount of
  explanation.

## The comparison they are making

When somebody says "I could buy a new one for that", they are not being rude. They are
comparing two things that look the same to them: a sofa, and a sofa.

Your job is to explain that they are not the same thing, without sounding defensive.

## Talk about the frame

The frame is the whole argument, and it is concrete enough that people follow it.

A mass-market sofa at the low end is stapled softwood or engineered board. It has a service
life of perhaps five to eight years and cannot be reupholstered economically, because there
is nothing solid to fix new work to. When it goes, it goes to landfill.

The piece in their living room, if it is worth recovering at all, has a jointed hardwood
frame. It has already lasted decades. Rebuilt properly it will last decades more, and can
be recovered again after that.

> "You're not buying a sofa. You're buying another thirty years out of the one you've
> already got, in exactly the fabric you want, in the size that fits the room."

That is the whole case, and it takes fifteen seconds.

## Talk about the hours, not the effort

Show the breakdown by all means, but present it as time, not as hardship.

A traditionally rebuilt wing-back is around twenty hours at the bench. Stripping, frame
repair, webbing, springs, stitched edges, calico, wadding, cutting, fitting, finishing.
The [cost estimator](/reupholstery-cost-calculator) will lay it out for you.

What you must not do is describe how difficult it is or how skilled you are. That reads as
pleading. Twenty hours is a fact; "it's really hard work" is an opinion the customer has no
way to evaluate.

## Show them instead

Most hesitation is not really about price. It is that the customer cannot picture the
result, and people do not spend serious money on something they cannot see.

This is the single biggest difference between winning and losing a quote. A fabric sample
the size of a beer mat asks somebody to imagine an entire sofa. Most people cannot do it,
so they delay, and a delayed decision usually becomes no decision.

## What never works

- Comparing yourself favourably to other upholsterers
- Explaining your overheads. It is not the customer's problem that your rent went up
- Dropping the price on the spot, which tells them the first number was invented
- Apologising for the figure before they have reacted to it

Say the number, then stop talking. The silence after a price feels much longer to the
person who said it than to the person hearing it.
